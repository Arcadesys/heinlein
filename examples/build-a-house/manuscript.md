title: "\"Build a House,\" She Said"
subtitle: An essay

There was a woman named Estelle who lived in Indianapolis. Ireland, too, before she was deported. She smoked like a chimney and she was absolutely, completely, off-her-rocker crazy. And she's my Mama - more so than any blood could claim.

She held my hand through transition. Introduced me to other trans people. Showed me what real power looked like - the kind that eats at institutions like termites in the walls until they create space for folks like us. She made sure I knew how to use a knife, and was always ready with a wild story about how she fucked her way around the world as a flight attendant.

Before we said our final goodbyes, she gave me a charge I did not agree to and could not put down.

Build a House.

---

Houses are never written into history because they were never supposed to exist. They shouldn't _have_ to exist, but they do.

A capital-H House, in this case, is a safe place for the transvestites. (It's an old term from a war older than language itself.) It is a place where the ones who got left could be gathered, named, corrected, fed, teased, defended, witnessed, and held.

The House held me while I cried about losing my family. It passed down the ancient, oral history of the community, the kind kept secret so the knowledge of transness would not be lost. It taught me how to disappear, to pass.

It taught me how to transform.

---

I came to transition in the aughts. Estelle had done so a decade before me. Together, we are links in a chain of oral history that survives through obscurity, tenacity, and desperation.

She was the gatekeeper at the clinic where I got my first shot of hormones. I learned from a trans friend about the only place in Indianapolis that would let me do it. I remember sitting there shaking like a leaf, feeling less like a patient than a suspect. I had wanted that shot so badly, had carried the wanting so long, and still some frightened part of me believed I had come to be tested. Measured. Found out. Sent home.

Years later, I learned that fear had not come from nowhere. The doctor trusted her to know who was trans and who was lying.

There is a whole history in that sentence. A whole architecture of scarcity and permission. A whole ugly little kingdom built around the idea that someone else had to recognize you before you could begin.

And still.

She saw me.

I do not know what she saw, exactly. Terror, probably. Need. Stubbornness. Some battered little ember of a self I could barely name without apologizing for it. Whatever it was, she saw enough. She opened the door. The needle went in. A future that had been theoretical became chemical, cellular, real.

---

She introduced me to others. We held each other through the hard times, lifted each other with gentle teasing and genuine awe at the act of each other's *becoming*. We learned the safe bars where we wouldn't get stares or worse. We met the local drag queens who made connections and community out of nothing. We scurried around enemy territory like rats, finding warmth where we could. And other than the rampant poverty, assaults, fear, humiliation, and murders that happened to our community at alarming rates, we survived, most of us.

It wasn't all sunshine. Life never is. Our traumas rubbed each other the wrong way. The fact was that, under everything, we were all humans from different lives surviving a painful, wonderful transformation. It is volatile. It is intensely beautiful. It burns. It brands.

Estelle was from the generation before mine. One of the women who learned to build shelter out of whatever the world had not managed to take yet. She'd been committed for wanting to be a girl. Shocked and tortured to try to take the desire away.

And yet she persisted. She protested, and sat in, and _fought_ until girls like her wouldn't have to suffer like she did. She held the quiet, defiant glamour and gall of existence, despite the costs. And she passed it along in the stories she told, the things she'd done, the places she'd been, the boys she blew.

The House carries room for those in transformation. A name said with conviction. A couch that became a bed. A dinner stretched past its budget. A hand on the back of someone who could not stop shaking. A quiet moment at a Shoney's where, for once, people didn't stare at the boy in a dress and I could just _be myself._

I have never known what to do with the fact that my first act of becoming passed through someone else's judgment. I have never known how to separate the harm of the gate from the mercy of the woman standing inside it. I have never known what to do with any of it.

That it was a trans person named Danni who saw I was close to taking my own life and pointed me toward the clandestine clinic where all the travesties in Indianapolis began their transition.

That it was a bunny on the internet who first put the word in my mouth: _I'm trans._ Not by force or persuasion, but by existing. By showing possibility: _no one can stop you from doing this._

That I _did this;_ despite all the anger, the rejection, the shame. The world tried to shove me in a box. I refused, and came out better for it.

I have never learned to forgive those who said, in hushed voices, "are you sure?" "Can't you just be gay?" "Think of your family." Which, to a writer, reads less as a wound to avoid and more like a challenge to overcome, so of course I wrote this.

The gall needed to survive was never supposed to have power. It was supposed to be snuffed out, pushed down. _Controlled._ But we survive anyway, and we understand our duty. That is the part nobody warns you about. The gate you fought to get through has a handle on the inside too.

I hold great sadness and great wisdom, gathered from those whom our society tried desperately to snuff out. It's grief and glamour; white-hot rage and the memory of those who aren't here anymore.

I wish I could separate them. I cannot. Maybe that is part of the debt.

---

She did not ask me whether I wanted the torch. She handed it to me anyway.

Or maybe handed is too gentle. She pointed. She pointed at the thing that needed building, then at me, and she kept pointing until I understood that refusal would not save me from the responsibility. People like that do not ask. They point. They keep pointing. Then they are gone, and the pointing stays in your chest like a splinter you cannot reach.

So yes. I carry a debt. I am paying it the only way I know how, which is to make something and put her fingerprints all over it without her permission. She gave me power the way she gave it to everyone: not by handing me anything, but by refusing to let me believe I had nothing. She taught me that, even at our lowest, we still have _something to give_.

That is the inheritance. Not a kingdom. A splinter and a job.

I did not understand that for a long time. I thought power was something handed down by people with names on buildings, money in accounts, signatures that opened doors. I thought power looked like permission. Protection. A clean inheritance. A key in the palm.

Not for people like us.

But a House does not begin with permission. A House begins when someone looks at the ruined materials of a life and says, Fine. We will build from this.

Estelle did not work like a benefactor. Estelle had nothing to hand down. She was not rich, not connected, not protected. What she had was an unbearable refusal to look away. She would find you in whatever state the world had left you in, point at the thing you carried, and keep pointing until you saw it too.

Power given to the powerless is mostly witness. It is being seen, hard and on purpose, until you stop arguing with the evidence.

That is what Houses have always known. Before a House can protect you, it has to see you. Not the version you make for paperwork. Not the version your family can tolerate. Not the version that apologizes for needing too much. You. The one under the costume and inside it. The one still choosing a name. The one who needs a place to stand while the rest of the world catches up or refuses to.

A House says: there you are.

Then it says: now come eat.

I have spent too much of my life letting pages say what my body could not. I do not judge that anymore. A page can keep you alive. It can carry the heat when your hands cannot. It can say the thing while you are still learning how to stand inside the sentence.

But survival is not the same thing as speech. A record is not the same thing as a reckoning. A letter can tell the truth and still keep the most human part of you outside the room.

That is one reason I keep turning to archives. Records are useful. Records can protect. Records can preserve a truth long enough for somebody else to find it. But records also have habits. They clean up blood. They turn panic into chronology. They make terror legible and grief respectable. They can hide behind their own accuracy.

I want my work to resist becoming a record. Not always successfully. Maybe no archive can. But I want the seams to show. I want the interviews and redactions and contradictions to keep arguing with the official story. I want the people inside the record to get their hands around the frame and shake it and say, "It was fucked up and it was beautiful and I wouldn't take back a second of it."

Because that is what family does. Not agreement. Not purity. Not a perfect account. Just the courage to keep talking and loving after the easy version has stopped being useful.

This is what I mean when I say I was told to build a House. Not an institution. Not a brand. Not a platform. A House. Something smaller and older and harder to explain to anyone who has never needed one. A House is not a charity. It is not rescue performed from a safe distance. It is kinship with chores. It is glamour with a grocery list. It is somebody yelling at you because they expect you to survive the night and call them in the morning.

It is love in a loveless world. A clipboard, a calling, and a cigarette burn in the table.

This is what trans resilience looks like. If the government burns down the forest we built, each of us carries a seed. That is the part they never understand: the knowledge travels.

She charged me with our history: not as a gift, but a burden. A failsafe.

The chain of this old knowledge remains unbroken through me. That may be the only real magic I believe in.

Which brings me, at last, to you.

You did not read an essay. You got conscripted.

That is how it works with this stuff. You witness the powerless taking what was always theirs, and now you cannot un-know that it can be done. The knowing is a small power, and a small power that goes unused curdles into the worst thing of all: despair.

So do not do that. Do not close this and feel moved and let the feeling evaporate by Tuesday. The power in these words was never the words themselves. It is in what you do after, in the smallest possible register: the person you decide to see and keep seeing, the thing you decide to make with your hands, the room you decide to open, the House you decide to build small enough to hold and stubborn enough to last.

She pointed at me.

Estelle pointed through me.

I am pointing at you.

Pass it on.

- Austen Tucker, House Arcade
