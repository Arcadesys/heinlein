## Broomsticks

_There wasn’t a lady in my graduating class who skipped out on Broomsticks. Nobody ever forgot the little ones who came off the street, hunched over as if trying to erase themselves from the world, and you were the only light in the middle of a deep, dark, tumbling sea._

~CB


Broomsticks shared a storefront with a tiny Pagan shop off a quiet stop of Chicago's Red Line. For most visitors the space contained bog-standard occult supplies: ritual books, incense, candles, even a shop cat named Lily who made friends with every new patron in the store. But if the stars were smiling on you, and you were tuned into the woven fabrics of magic that pulsed just out of sight, you'd find yourself at the premier magick shop of Chicago, where the needy could find the services of an actual, real-life Witch for a decent price. For some, this existed in the realm of fantasy and fiction. For Aria, on the other hand, this was just a work study gig for college. Salma offered constant shifts that respected her class schedule, with plenty of time to study for her Colonial-era Magic class, and on occasion she'd get to tweak the hell out of a bewildered stoner that stumbled through the veil to find themselves lost in a world beyond their own reality. Win-win for everyone, really.

But when the teen walked into the store; wearing a thrift-store androgynous top, hoodie, girl jeans, and a conveniently-not-quite-a-purse shoulder bag; Aria definitely took notice. It was in the way they shuffled through the store, head tilted to ratty rugs that covered distressed wood floors and dust-covered poultices on the bottommost shelves. It was in the way their arms crossed tight over the body, the way they jumped when floorboards creaked under their feet, the way they hugged the walls when they walked. Always on edge, ready to bolt at the slightest hint of disapproval. They ran their fingers over racks of potions and scrolls that promised good luck, beauty, treasure from trash, and all of them may as well have been pearls before swine, for all the teen cared.

Aria's eyes lit up as the kid moved toward the counter. Six weeks of work at Broomsticks, mostly spent watching dust collect in the corners, was about to pay off. She closed her textbook with a snap that echoed off the hardwood walls. She beamed as the kid jumped and yelped at the sound.

"Welcome to Broomsticks! We have all the answers you seek, child. Just relax and let me get to work - don't worry about a thing!"

The teen jumped, looked to the door. "I'm sorry," they said, voice trembling. "I was looking for a different shop. I'll go."

Aria shook her head. "You found what you needed, child. Sit tight." She reached under the counter, rustled through her bag, and produced a tiny wooden keychain charm in the shape of a Witch hat. Two taps on the brim and a black, leather pointed hat erupted from the keychain. She took a moment to fit it on her head before she continued to look the kid down.

"Now, child. Let me have a look--oh! You look like someone who wants to disappear. No surprise, given that you found this place."

The teen shrugged the hoodie higher up on their shoulders. Aria swept a lock of brown hair under her hat, walked out from behind the old, ornate wooden shop counter, all smiles, and reached out her hands. She thought about what her parents would have made of all this: their darling boy in a jet black shift dress and Witch hat talking to a kid lost in their own gender so that they could be set on the right path. They'd have screamed and moaned, probably. Maybe even called on the local churches to protest.

Good thing she cut contact two years ago, in any case.

"My name's Aria. What's yours?"

"Charlie." It came out in a whisper. The kid couldn't have been older than fifteen, but the weariness in their hazel eyes suggested an old soul with too much to think about.

"Don't worry. Nobody's going to hurt you here. Come over to the table and have a seat."

"You don't know that," Charlie said. Their body tensed, eyes focused on the door.

"I do, actually. I'll prove it."

Aria walked to the door. Outside, two women in yoga pants and sipping on bubble tea pointed to things through the storefront window. They laughed to a joke only they could hear and Charlie shrank away from their glare.

Then, before Charlie could get any more freaked out, Aria started banging on the door. She screamed: "Hey, yuppie scum! There's two people in here about to talk about gender feels!"

The ladies sipped their tea and walked on, oblivious. Aria walked back toward the table as Charlie stared, jaw agape. "Relax! Most people see a little Pagan store here when they walk past. Nice folks, all told, and their cat is the sweetest thing. You get to Broomsticks for two reasons: one, you're so altered on drugs or alcohol that you forget how to stay in the bounds of reality; or two, you're one of Estelle's Children."

"Estelle's Children?"

Aria shrugged. "In tune with magic, I mean."

Charlie shook their head. "So this store is the real deal."

"Magic as heck," Aria said with a smile. She slid out two chairs from a low-slung wooden table at the rear of the shop. The walls flanked her with the accouterments of Witches of old: broomsticks, wands, fungi, the occasional brown bottle of _something strange_; and to really send the message home Aria willed the lights to dim, candles to light. "Real Magic. Real Witches. Charlie, come have a seat."

Charlie marched over to the table and took a seat - they always did, once she started doing magic in front of them. She took a seat on the chair closest to the wall and produced a small cosmetic mirror from a drawer behind the counter.

"I thought magic wasn't real," Charlie said.

"I get that a lot," Aria said with a chuckle. She pushed the mirror toward Charlie; they diverted their eyes the second their face came into view. "You look like you're on the verge of a gender meltdown, if I can be so bold."

"How did you know?"

"I've worn that look," Aria said, shrugging. "Pretty shirt hiding under a big, baggy hoodie; jeans that you can excuse as 'they look better on me than boy jeans.' Shaggy hair that _just so happens_ to look good under that girly headband. Someone told you that you shouldn't dress femme. Maybe they ridiculed you. Maybe they punished you. But you can't face it straight on - not out here, where the public can see you! If I was a betting woman I'd say that bag on your shoulder has the dress that made you realize you aren't who you thought you were. Something... hmm. Something practical. Down-to-earth enough that you feel less like a drag queen and more like the girl next door. Something that made you believe you could actually be a girl."

Charlie pressed the bag closer to their body and blushed.

"And that one's not magic, dear. That's just years of experience talking. I know that look. I know what you need."

They slipped the shoulder bag onto the back of a chair and slinked deeper into their seat. "You're not a cop, I'm guessing."

"Runaway?"

Charlie nodded. Aria nodded and waved a finger in the air. A clamor rose from the back room of Broomsticks as a sentient pair of oven mitts went about brewing tea and making sandwiches. Her guest jumped at the noise, but Aria calmed Charlie with a wink. "Snacks," she said, smiling. "I figured you may be hungry."

"I... uh." Charlie's face flushed. "Thanks. None of this is going to turn me into, like, a frog or anything, right?"

Aria shrugged. "Only if you want it to."

"I came here on Amtrak," she continued. "I told a friend I wanted to be a girl and, well, it got back to Mom. She was furious. Wanted to send me to a camp to 'make me better.' I didn't know what to do so I just headed to Boystown. Figured it'd be the safest place for a weirdo like me, you know?"

"A lot of kids believe that." It was a story common in the community; some downstater kid realizes they'll never fit in with their hometown, and they are left with limited options. Suicide, the closet, or start over in the one place every queer kid in Illinois knows accepts people like them. "Pain pushes you to make decisions like that."

Charlie nodded. "There weren't any beds at The Crib. I figured I'd hang out in the common room until they kicked me out but then this older lady told me to come here. Said I'd know when to get off the train and where to find the place, and... and I _did_."

Aria smiled. "Estelle is a peach."

Charlie's eyes widened. "So... so she's like you?"

"Like _us_, Charlie. Grew up hiding our dresses in the deepest parts of our closet. All that separates us is time in transition."

"But aren't you a Witch?"

Aria winked at them. "It's just time in transition, Charlie. Are you okay with Charlie? And is it okay if I use they/them for you?"

Charlie nodded so hard that their hair flopped in front of their eyes.

"Charlie is a nice name," Aria said. "And it's okay not to know who you are from time to time."

It was at that point the oven gloves flew from the back of the room. Between them, a tea tray filled with finger sandwiches and a fresh pot of hot tea. To her credit Charlie didn't make much noise about the magical kitchen crew past thanking the gloves before they flew back to clean up.

Charlie fell into the food. Aria took the opportunity to pull out the mirror and face it in their direction. When the teen looked up they were faced with their own reflection, and the sight sent them ducking under the table.

Aria gave them time to recover before tapping on the mirror's frame and pushing it closer. "When you look in this mirror, what do you see?"

"Myself."

"Who is that?"

They stuffed another bite of sandwich in their mouth and talked around it. "I see a failure of a boy. I'm... I'm not sure what I am, really. Big chin, Adam's apple the size of a small nectarine, bushy eyebrows. But it's like... like the person in that mirror isn't me?"

Aria produced a small vial from her pocket. "Drink this and try again."

Their eyes went wide. "Is this a magic potion?"

Aria shrugged. "This mirror shows anyone who gazes upon it their truest self. The potion helps facilitate that. So go on: drink, and look again."

Charlie gulped down the vial without hesitation and stared into the mirror like a person possessed. Second by second their face began to light up with intense joy. Then laughter came: first as a chuckle, then a giggle, until they were howling with joyous, hand-on-forehead, disbelieving laughter.

They picked up the mirror to get a better look at themself. "I see a beautiful person. I'm wearing this darling red sundress and my hair is up in a high ponytail. I'm not sure if I'm a boy or a girl, but it almost doesn't matter. It's just... just _me_ there.

"Dear god, this is the first time I've seen myself. Ever." They put down the mirror and looked Aria with wide eyes. "What in the heck was in that potion?"

Aria let the question hang on the air for a while. Charlie leaned in, eyes growing wider. They'd been poisoned! Cursed! This was how it all ended, right here in this dingy shop, and nobody would know how their life had ended--

Then, before Charlie could work into a full panic, Aria let out a laugh. "Relax! It's sugar water, dear. Nothing changed. You weren't letting yourself see what was in the mirror. And it's... this won't be an overnight thing, okay? It's like watching a car crash in reverse and in slow motion at the same time. But I think I can help you."

"But what if my parents find me?"

"They may," Aria said, shrugging. "If you have a phone they bought then they may have already started tracking your movements. Police help, sometimes. And you don't deserve to feel unsafe at home, either; you deserve to feel respected, safe, and loved.

"Listen. If I were you - and I'm not you - I'd figure out some way to make it work at home. Hide in the closet for a few more years. Make a plan. Take a summer job and save up cash. The second you turn eighteen you give 'em the finger and sign up for classes at Salma."

"Salma?"

"Witching school," Aria said. Then she reached for Charlie's forehead, pressed a thumb between her eyes, and pushed through the veil that covered their eyes. It parted and the pair were floating past reality, galaxies swirling past them like grains of sand in an ocean, and as they floated a long, unbroken tapestry of glowing blue light rose up to embrace them both. Charlie was there, red sundress and high ponytail and all, and her body swirled with magical energy that swirled between fingertips, pulsed with each heartbeat.

"I'm a Witch."

"Welcome to the Sisterhood," Aria said, and then released her thumb from Charlie's forehead. As quickly as it began, it ended; their bodies released from the Loom below like stones from a slingshot. Galaxies flew past them beyond the speed of light; Broomsticks rebuilt itself around them one piece of wood at a time; and in the middle of it all Charlie found themselves unwinding back into their real-world body, hoodie and all.

Charlie sat at the table, arms outstretched to keep their balance, dazed. Aria pushed away, walked to the counter, and produced a small bag decorated with white, pink, and blue ribbons. She placed it on the table in front of Charlie and began pulling out trinkets: a wooden keychain hat, a "so you're trans" brochure, resources for therapy, a twenty-ounce bottle of pink potion, and a leather-bound spellbook entitled "Fundamentals of Magic."

"I can't take you any further, Charlie. We may want to help but harboring runaways is still against the law. But this will help. Read the books. Take a teaspoon of the potion each day. If you run out you can make more - the spell is in your book and only uses pantry staples. And if your parents ever take this away from you, well, I'll make sure there are copies of everything in your Citadel."

"My Citadel?"

"Your little plot of land outside of reality," Aria said. She stood from the table and offered a hand to Charlie. Together they walked to the back of the store, where a single glowing door stood out against dust-covered boxes in a storage closet. "You can always escape there if you close your eyes and imagine the door in your mind.

"We'll be neighbors there; feel free to knock on the big, rainbow door after you settle in; I'll be there and ready to help. I love doing makeovers, too, if that's something that interests you."

Aria didn't need an answer for that; Charlie's beaming smile spoke volumes. <!-- BREATHE: the smile is already the answer. "spoke volumes" tells the reader what they just saw. Cut the second clause? -->


"And again, you're probably going to get found. Child Protective Services might get involved. But this Citadel, these tools, this gift… they should help you fight back. And the second you get your feet, and your eighteenth birthday comes around, well, you run to us."

"I don't know what to say,"

Aria put a finger to Charlie's lips. "Eighteenth birthday, Charlie. Calculate the number and burn it into your heart. Remember your truth. Retreat to your Citadel when you need to. I'll always be right next door when you arrive, ready to talk. But for now, you plan is to survive. Understand me?"

They nodded. Aria pointed them toward the door and they walked to it. Their hand was on the handle when they turned back to ask one last question.

"Lady Estelle Extravaganza, dear. Same lady who talked to you at The Crib tonight did that for me five years ago. She's done that for most everyone I know at Salma. Soon, when you find your feet in this world, you'll do the same thing.

## A Note from the Archive

I never knew my mother. But if you ask me about my Mother, I'll tell you the story of Lady Estelle Extravaganza, the fiercest Witch who ever lived.

Lady Estelle Extravaganza died in 2120, a hundred and fifty years old, aided by both magic and the love of the sisterhood she had built from the ashes of the post-Plague years with little more than empathy and an open door. Over the course of the next century she would teach the Witching ways to hundreds of trans folk who wandered through her modest magic parlor in sleepy Ravenswood, Chicago. They came: rich, poor, destitute, abused, loved, sometimes with nothing left to lose. No matter what there was always a hot bed and a shoulder to cry on in Estelle's house before she'd lay down the tea: "Cryin's over. Let's work on the rest of your life."

It is fitting that Estelle passed on the fifteenth anniversary of Sylvia Rivera Day. She was never one to pass up an opportunity to come through. I had the pleasure of attending her pre-death kiki in hospice, surrounded by a sisterhood I only knew in part. Scores of women came from all around the world, all lined up to hold her hand one last time.

It was there, buffeted by the harsh, mechanical whine of an oxygen machine and an IV drip hissing into her body, that she produced the thick, leather-bound volume of handwritten pages from under her pillow, her eyes alight with fire. "The family history," she said with a weak, sparkling smile, and pushed my hands tight to the embossed cover. "I know you'll put it to good use."

Inside were her personal journals, printed-off email chains, handwritten letters, postcards, photos, maps of the magical ley-lines that crisscrossed the land under our feet. A hundred years of magic, all of it kept in her own hand. I have embellished many of the tales — flair and panache the page may not allow — but I think she would have wanted it that way.

Lady Estelle Extravaganza may have been meek and friendly, always happy to meet you halfway, but make no mistake: she raised an army, one awakened queer person at a time. She turned down gifts, gratitude, even positions of authority; choosing instead to end the ritual with a gentle smile, hands on shoulders, and eyes steeled with purpose. From her lips, a simple mandate:

"The cryin's over. Welcome to the Sisterhood: pass it on."

The cryin's just starting for us. But amidst our celebration and our grief, I invite you to remember the tears we shed, the laughs we shared, and the bonds we formed as Estelle's children.

It is now my turn to pass these on to you.



In the name of the Sisterhood,
Lady Claire Belfast
Curator, National Magic Archives

## Admissions Day

Every year, at the height of Midsummer, the secretive halls of Salma's School for Witches opened its doors for admissions. It was a converted convent from an old church that had long since been converted into a meeting space. Not a lot of people knew about us, but those who did would line up around the yard, their hands on the shoulders of their gender-questioning kids. Sometimes you'd see an old trans person standing in the line, dressed like they were going to an interview for a top-secret job. Some had friends who had access to the Loom; others had heard whispers from their friends in the community, encouraging them to show up on some strange cross-street in Chicago's Edgewater neighborhood. The prize: access to infinite cosmic power through the Divine Loom that bound us all.

I stumbled into the middle of all these hopes and dreams, decked to the nines in plum-colored dress robes and pointy hat, leading with my shoulder through the dozens of people. Mothers lifted their children to me; teenagers with ragged clothes bent down below the brim of my hat to beg, plead for an admissions ticket.

They all looked to me with stars in their eyes. The robes. The hat. The stature of a trans woman standing tall, proud. A Witch, with all the powers and privileges provided therein, and they wanted to be just like me.

"You're late."

Lady Estelle stood at the door, waving and flashing me this big smile that reeked of "performative niceness." She stood at the top of the stoop, her straight white hair peeking out from under the brim of a black, pointed hat, and beckoned for me to join her. The crowd cheered; we held hands and waved to them before ducking inside.

Once the door was closed I could see Salma bustling with activity. Green-robes set out concessions for the crowd and set about giving a spinet piano the sentience of a jazz player. A handful of older women, all old friends, stood in curtained-off stalls, a box of testing materials at the ready. Me, I wouldn't be joining them. My job was far less… well, less _delicate_ than helping some cute kids discover their magical powers for the first time. And I'm sure Estelle would rather have anybody else do that job, if I weren't so dang good at it.

She took me by the shoulders and reached into her left sleeve, producing a pack of mints. She shook it at me. "You smell like whisky."

I took the mints, scoffing. "Did I ever mention that there's this tut-tutting look on your face you reserve only for me? Someone curses your name in front of your face? Stone faced indifference. Someone murders a trans woman in the streets? Compassion and understanding. I have a few drinks the night before, and it's that face."

Estelle smacked me on the shoulder. "You're supposed to be meeting _children_ today, Gertrude. At least pretend to be a little respectful."

Estelle walked me to the window and nodded to a boy and his father near the front of the crowd. Stepford type, to be sure; father in the vest and button-up, his hand on his son's shoulder, squeezing just too tightly. No wife to speak of, but you could see a divot where the ring used to be on his finger. The boy just looked like he wanted to disappear.

The Dad was uncomfortable - not with money or opportunity, but the place itself. The sound of Witches' voices. Taking orders from proud, up-front trans women darting around in black robes, firmly in control.

"Luddite, I take it?"

"Worse. Opportunist."

I closed my eyes, focused hard on keeping the smile bright, wide. "They sound just darling, Estelle. Why can't I work the runaway line at one of these events?"

"Come now."

"You know. The shaking, vulnerable little dears, all crying out for makeovers, love. adoration. Like..."

"Don't start—"

"Like me," I said, leaning my hand on her shoulder. "Way back when. When we were friends. When you saved my ass from the dark place."

I was 17 years, 11 months, and 2 days old when I first learned I could make magic. It was - god, I can't believe I'm saying this - it was a _mail-order magic kit_ that got me in touch with Estelle, back when Salma was a handful of Witches making the rounds on local late night shows and showing the extent of their powers but still subjected to the indignity of having their deadnames and deadpictures shown on air, to the hushed gasps and whispers of a studio audience who just couldn't believe that a boy could possibly turn out to be a beautiful, powerful woman.

I grew up in a little corn-grid town that once played host to the most senior members of the KKK, and the history cast a long shadow across the town. All-white, and all-American, this was the kind of place where a boy was supposed to become a man and a girl was to blossom into a fragile, tender princess, usually of corn or pumpkins or pigs or whatever-the-heck else theme county fairs decided to set for their pageants. I was _less female_ than I preferred to be at the time, and hiding the evidence from everyone was taking its toll. People started asking questions. Offering SSRIs. Coming to call at odd hours just to "make sure I was okay," and stealing quick glances into my closet as they left my room.

(Gosh, it's hard to even remember a time when trans women weren't something that was respected and feared in the community. Thirty years ago we were the boogeyman politicians could conjure to stir up votes. Forty years ago you had to know someone who knew someone to even get treated at all. Nowadays you can buy perfectly-functional girl-juice anywhere in Chicago without a prescription, among other things. I hear make-your-fursona mixing stations are really taking off in the 'burbs now - one of my old students is probably on the way to making millions off the idea as I write this story.

It's a far cry from what I found when I came home from school that day. A dress - my dress, the dress I'd pinched from my sister's hamper, the dress I hid in an old gym bag tucked behind a pile of old books in the back of my closet - neatly folded on the bed, a note pinned to it.

"Found in your closet. Don't leave. We'll talk when your father and I get home."

So yeah. There's that.)

I locked every door I could - the front door, the back door, garage, my bedroom, both sides of the Jack-and-Jill bathroom I shared with my sister, even the window in the bathroom. I'd even gone so far as to barricade up my bedroom door on the off-chance that the they got home early. I was alone in the bathroom, stowing the box in the bottom of the linen closet, heart pounding out of my chest. I had hours - minutes, maybe - before I had to have this terrible, terrible talk. I laid out each component from the box on the back of the toilet: a vial of clear fluid, a pinch of flash paper, a placard titled "MAKE FIRE," obviously printed on some budget inkjet printer short on red ink. It showed a collection of hand silhouettes walking through some ritual. If I heard a door slam I could dump the whole mess down the drain, flush, and get ready to deny the shit out of everything that happened.

Step one: put paper in vial. Close eyes. Breathe. Imagine a safe place, a place beyond places, a place only I could visit. I panicked, thought of the single-stall restroom outside the cafeteria: the one that nobody checked and had a solid lock, where I could sit to pee without the boys making fun of me. (It... it sounds silly now, I know. My safe place was a high school bathroom. You took what you could get back then.)

Arms front. Up. Out. A clap of the hands, catharsis, silence.

Then: warmth.

I opened my eyes and turned off the lights. There, dim and blue, a single flame licked the rim of the vial. My heart caught in my throat. I read the paper:

"IN CASE OF FIRE, CONGRATULATIONS! YOU'RE A FLAMING, QUEER TRANS WITCH. CALL ME, SISTER!"

That's when I heard the garage door open.

Estelle set me up in a small office for that faced out onto the garden. It was cozy, to put it lightly; a desk, three chairs, and a little side table where I could set up test supplies. I laid out a vial and a little square of flash paper while the Stepford father looked on, impatiently, a smile wearing thin on his face. Sure, most Witches nowadays went with the more accurate tests: spit in a cup, add a drop of estradiol and a swirl of Detecterine, swirl, wait for the mixture to glow. But I was a sucker for the theatrics when the troublesome parents came knocking.

"Just a moment while I set up," I said. I spoke straight to the kid, a waifish little thing with shoulder-length blonde hair, skin-and-bone arms, gaunt face that must not have seen a smile in its entire existence.

"Apologies. You are my first applicant for the day, friend. I am Lady Gertrude, but you can call me Gertrude." I smiled wider at the child. "Trudy, if you're a friend."

The Dad marched straight over and reached for a firm handshake. His eyes locked on mine, a too-wide smile on his face. He smelled of burning aftershave, fresh linen. His hair reminded me of a hedgehog who got loose in a drawer full of grease.

"It's just a miracle, Lady Gertrude. My boy Hallan can _do magic_!"

He clapped Hallan on the shoulder. The boy was maybe a hundred pounds soaking wet, wearing a long flannel shirt and form-fitting jeans. His nametag: "My name is HALLAN," written with the perfect penmanship most teens lacked, and by the way he crossed his arms over the tag I guessed it wasn't his first choice. He lifted his head to meet my gaze, opened his mouth to speak, and then found himself shaking as his father squeezed tighter and rocked him from side to side.

"That's my boy Hallan for you. The first warlock in history!" Dad, chuckling. Dad, lifting his hands from Hallan's shoulders to grip at his vest with both hands. "Least, that'd be what I call male Witches. I'm sure coining that word's above my pay grade."

I smiled a wide, fake smile at him. "I think that's Hallan's decision, if it comes to it."

Dad leaned in close, the reek of aftershave burning my nostrils. "Of course, I'd be interested in any remote learning opportunities you have to offer, my Lady. Hallan may have strong magic but he's got such a weak constitution. Gets homesick at the drop of a hat, poor thing!"

"We have a generous weekend visitation policy at Salma. There are plenty of Doorwomen on staff who can close the distance between any two points in the blink of an eye. Any student who wishes to see their family can do so at any time—"

"We could use his help at home, too. Family business. I do hope you understand. Folks like us — folks without magic, you see — we have to work to feed our family."

I gestured for Hallan to come closer, and when the father moved to follow I shooed him away. "Just the child, please," I said, firmer this time.

Hallan slinked over with intentional, slow steps. Shoulders slumped, eyes pointed at the floor, he took an eternity to cover the four feet to my table. As he walked he kept one eye over his shoulder, watching for guidance from his father's grim, stern glare.

(I made sure to catch Dad's eye and glare, hard, my hand slipping from under the robe to show a glint of burning energy. _This will be a private conversation_, I said with my eyes, and he got the message.)

Once Hallan was seated I put a copy of Estelle's old instructions into his hands, making sure to squeeze hands as I did so. Tiny, gentle pulses of calming energy licked from my fingers and into his shaking hands. He looked to me, jaw agape, and I just winked in return.

"Let's try this again: hello, Hallan. My name is Gertrude. Is it okay if I call you Hallan? Would you prefer another name?" His lips curled in and his head started turning back toward his father. I took his cheek in one hand before he could make eye contact.

"I didn't ask your father, dear. I asked you. Do you have another name you prefer? It can be anything. I promise you that I won't laugh, and your father will never, _ever_ bring it up to you again." (I said that part _loud_, my eyes darting to Dad as I said it.)

The kid took a breath that caught in his throat. They reached out for my hand and gave it a squeeze. Exhaled. He sat up rod-straight, eyes meeting mine, possessed by unassailable truth.

"Haley," they said. Then, again, louder this time: "You can call me Haley."

When people hear the phrase "Witching School" they get grand ideas in their heads. Cavernous spaces built upon centuries of tradition, where night is day and up is down and all that mess. ## Jenny Appleseed


The campgrounds attached to the Hancock, IN County Fair were usually a placid affair. As far as the eye could see there were trailers full of country folk here to show their best ears of corn, the hum of country music from tabletop radios, and the crunch of cheap beer cans being tossed into campfires. But for one night in August it transformed - just for half an instant - into a place of real and genuine magic. It started when Jenny Appleseed pulled up in her van - all six feet of her frame, stocky from a life of farmhand work that happened a whole gender ago - and set to drawing a crowd with long, bright streamers made of pure light. They twisted up in the air, red and white, a personal and portable big top to house the greatest one-woman show on Earth. The ex-farmhand turned Witch extraordinaire, complete with a bule, pink, and white mohawk and a jean jacket that would have made Hedwig's Angry Inch quite jealous.

Carmen had seen it before and would see it a dozen times more on her trip out of Chicago. For a final exam about "passing the gift on" they'd done a lot of dancing and very little to find other trans folk to become Witches. She didn't fit in with the sweet smell of dirt and the jeans-and-t-shirt chic of the campground; preferring a simple A-line dress, cats-eye makeup, and a little clutch for good measure. Pure, cool city chic, all the way down; she may have found her way into the country, but she'd be damned if the country found its way into her.

She pulled a chair from the back of the van and kicked it out beside the pile of instruments on the ground. Jenny walked around the front of the van and put a hand on Carmen's shoulder. She snapped her fingers and a long, sheer rainbow dress fell over her like a waterfall. She let go and the dress traced lazy circles around her body as she twirled to the beat. And then there she was, under the awning of an illusory big top, all smiles and open arms toward a crowd. For anyone else this would have been a once-a-year, Pride Week kind of show. For Jenny Appleseed, however, it was Tuesday.

The pair cast a mindspeak spell so that what one thought about saying, the other heard.

Jenny shouted to the crowd. "Welcome, one and all, to my Hootenanny!"

Carmen sneered. "Hootenanny? Really?"

Jenny looked ot her and winked. "You have to lean in if you want to win the crowd, Carmen. Get folks talkin' an' trustin', and then we can suss out who needs to find their Citadel. Just like we did in Jennings County!"

Carmen laughed and threw her hands up in frustration. "It's been five days and we've only introduced three women to the loom."

Jenny shrugged. "Stop thinking about numbers! Remember how we spent the day fixing teeth in Jennings? Remember how Hannah's family was so thankful for the free care that they didn't even mind when she broke the news?"

"Still."

She shook her head. "And it's not just a numbers game, baby. We're out here doing good!"

Carmen opened her mouth to talk but Jenny shook her head and put a finger to her lips. "Showtime." She raised her hands like a conductor and counted in the instruments laying on the floor. Then, to the crowd, Jenny hollered. No. _Yodeled,_ even if Jenny insisted there was a difference between the two. She howled like a dog and the crowd yodeled right back at her.

"Let's get to dancin', people!"

It began with a drum circle, a banjo, and a fiddle roaring to life under the canopy of her panel van, floating in thin air as they played a wicked, quick bluegrass tune. Then Jenny came to life. She moved as if possessed by the rhythm, dancing folksy circles in the middle of an empty camp lot. She came on as a force of nature that refused to be ignored, and as the instruments rose to a crescendo she showed the extent of her power.

Fireworks shot from her fingers and drew arcs of prismatic light across the sky. The smell of harvest markets and the burn of powerful moonshine blew through on a cool, autumn breeze - a godsend in Indiana's late summer heat. Scarecrows tumbled from the van and reached for her arm and they wove tight circles of arm-locked dancing. Carmel apples and fresh bottles of beer floated through the air, searching for a sweet tooth or a dry mouth in the crowd. And the crowd came: smiling, laughing, clapping to the beat.

In the middle of it all, a single child watched Jenny with rapt attention, laser focus, and jaw on the floor. Sure, the crowd was whoopin' and hollerin' up a storm, squinting to make sense of the show through too many beers, but this child might as well have been seeing the Holy Ghost themself.

"That's the one," Jenny whispered. The swirling rainbow energies over her head coalesced into a single, maypole-like mass that ducked through the earth, flaring up through a fire, until it finally hanged over the child's head, sprinkling down confetti and fanfare.

"One Witch." Carmen laughed. Some of the onlookers shot her a sour stare; everyone else was clapping along and bouncing to the beat but Carmen sat in her city-slicker chic, just sulking. "We could hit five GSAs in high schools in a day. Call up drop-in youth shelters and offer to volunteer. We could do ten, twenty of these kids in a single day."

"Word'll travel that far on its own. Some witch gets done with Salma, they'll head home and spread it just out of duty. You know what Estelle says--"

"--When you meet them, you'll just _know_."

Jenny smiled her way. "This is how I meet the ones I know."

She reached a hand out to the kid. The scarecrows bowed; the drums rolled; and the crowd held their breath in eager anticipation. The child wore a ball cap, long brown hair, baggy jeans, and a t-shirt that hid the slightness of their frame. Looking at them you couldn't tell at a glance if they were a tomboy or a very lithe man. They watched the proceedings with hands across their chest, sneering at the silly dancing and the little tricks going on around them.

Behind, a couple in lawn chairs looked on with smiles on their face. The man, dressed in flannel and ripped blue jeans, was too old for his years.

He tipped a bottle toward the child and gave a nod. "Well, Jolene, don't keep the nice lady waitin'."

The dance kicked up in earnest. The band took up a country tune and the scarecrows started a line dance with the crowd. The audience peeled off, one by one, to join the scarecrows as they learned the dance. Carmen felt herself lift out of the chair as the chorus picked up and before she knew what was happening she was learning the 16-step alongside everyone else.

She almost gave herself over to the music completely but Jenny's voice kept her grounded:

"--We have little time. I can only keep up this level of fanfare for a minute or two. I'm sorry, but I have to ask a lot of questions."

The kid nodded.

"First off, what do you think of the dancing instruments and the scarecrows? Magic, ain't it?"

The child shrugged and spoke. Carmen would have given her left arm to hear her half of the conversation.

"You see me pulling their strings out of the air? You've always felt out of place here, haven't you? Like an ice cube on a humid day, or a chicken in a fox den."

The couple in the chairs looked on at the impromptu line dance. But when they looked back to Jenny, eyes soured with apprehension, Carmen leaped into action. She peeled away from the dance and, mid-stride, molded her body into that of a cardinal. Nobody seemed to notice with the college kid melted into a rather large red bird, took flight, and landed in front of the couple, pointing back at the line dance.

"May I have this dance?" she asked, wings flapping. The couple jumped back in a fit of shock. It wasn't every day a giant bird asked you to dance, after all.

Jenny, noticing the clamor, leaned in close to Jolene. "This is our gift, Jolene. The gift for those who don't see ourselves in the mirror. The boys who wanted to be girls, girls who wanted to be boys, the _Us-es_ of this world. The gift of the Sisterhood, passed down through generations."

"But I'm not a girl," Jolene said. "I want to be a boy!"

Jenny chuckled. "Sisterhood, brotherhood, whatever; it's just a word, child. What's important here is that this power is your birthright. I'm here to help you claim it."

"I can do magic?"

Jenny nodded. Carmen, meanwhile, kept flapping her wings, trying to block Jolene's parents from getting curious. She heaved from the effort - humans don't usually flap their arms this hard, and those transmutation spells did nothing for the muscles.

_Lots of work for one kid,_ she thought.

"My boy, you _are_ magic." Jenny touched the heel of her thumb to Jolene's forehead. Carmen felt a wave of energy cresting over her, cold and fuzzy and full of possibility. A new Witch, born out of the kind of love that bound strangers over the common cause of their bodies, their doubts, and their dreams.

Jenny took Jolene by the shoulders with a wide grin. "The cryin's over, Jolene. Welcome to the Sisterhood; Pass it on."

The band roared to a crescendo. The sunset above blotted out to moonlit sky; the scarecrows flew up to the heavens, spiraling until they disintegrated into loose haystacks that fell like country rain on the folks below. Then: ear-ringing silence followed, centered around Jenny and Jolene, bowing to each other, hands in hands.

Jenny's voice boomed in her head. "Take note of the technique, Carmen. Next town we get to, you're up. And grab a few Meal Seeds from storage; It's about time we get to helping these people."

When Jolene and her family took to their car Jenny walked over and, with little more than a smile and a few kind words, convinced them to let Carmen and Jenny park their van in their lawn for a spell.

"That had to be magic," Carmen said as Jolene walked back to the van.

Jenny shrugged "Just kindness, dear, and an offer to do some handiwork. You'd be shocked how far that'd get you out here."

Within a few weeks these seeds would grow into mulberry trees whose fruits would last a month in the fridge and feed an adult for a whole day with a single berry. It wouldn't replace food in the community but at least none would go hungry. The pastor kicked up a fuss, sure, but the farmer that owned the lot next door was all too happy to trade a community berries for the cash these trees would bring in. Jenny worked a spade into the ground at ten-foot intervals; Carmen planted a single berry in each hole; and behind them Jolene would place a bit of manure before covering the hole with topsoil from a wheelbarrow.

The church windows were wide open, and from the building they could hear the gentle, lifting chorus of a hymn. ## Familiar Apprentice

The dirty secret of transmogrification magic is that it’s all based on curses.

“You didn’t need to do that,” I stammered. I ran my hands against the exposed metal of the patient table and felt sickly-green bolts leave me, hurrying toward the ground from which they came. “I know what needs done.”

“I need you here with me.”

“You need me to keep my hands on her temples. You fire off the spell, I catch the negative effects. Don’t worry - I got this. Not hard to take a hit from a curse.”

“It’s not that.”

“It’s exactly that,” I said, shrugging. “That’s the job I’m here to do.”

“I mean...” Her face screwed into a scowl. “Never mind, Haley. The music was distracting, is all.”

“Do you have any requests?” I offered. “If we’re going to spend the next hour mind-linked I can at least make it pleasant.”

“White noise.”

“Party pooper.”

Estelle laid hands on Megan and pushed, hard, through her body. We three fell through the world, down through the floor, the foundation, the sewers, the salt of the earth, until we were drifting in a void of our own making. Glittering ley lines extended out in all directions, forming constellations that darted across the horizon. I looked to Lady Estelle and saw a shimmering paean of beauty: a bright jade sunrise over cherry blossoms and giggling babies, the smell of rosemary wafting on a breeze, the touch of an angel.

And silence.

I always hoped the supernatural world would have a taste for music. Something Bjork would like, maybe, with strange rhythms and haunting choruses, but out past our reality there was just a bunch of nothing. Silence as far as the ear could hear. I opened my mouth to speak but nothing came. I belted the lines to a song from Rent - you know, the one about going out tonight - but all that came forth was a wisp of green mana that faded into the oblivion around me.

Focus on the ritual, I reminded myself. Peaceful and surreal as it may have been the world beyond our world wasn’t kind to wandering minds. I looked down to Megan and let my hands rest on her cheeks, palmis at her temples. Estelle's energy flowed into her and I felt prickles of the magical runoff flowing white-hot into my wrists. She glared at me as she worked, her eyes furrowing when I lost focus, toe kicking me when I forgot to center my own energies... a doting mother I never really had the pleasure of knowing. I flashed her a warm smile; she glared in return.

Below, Megan became the woman she always knew she was. Years melted away; curves softened; and the core of her being, long scarred over by a thousand cuts of growing up a trans woman before the mainstreaming of magic, became subsumed by Estelle’s aura. Swaths of her life callused over with new memories: a prom dress where there once was a badly-fitting tux and barely-contained tears; a woman-only college instead of her tiny religious school; a new first job, a new first date, a new... a new everything, laid out with all the precision and rigor of a craftswoman completely in her element.

Meanwhile, my world gave way to patterns. Calico, first and foremost: soft white fur, splotched in black and brown, covering my body. The prick of two ears peeking up from my pixie cut. A tail brushing against the legs of the table. Claws digging into the cloth, scratching deep marks down the fabric, so satisfying with each pass of my paws.

And as it happened I found myself staring at Estelle with awe. I reached out to her with one paw, claws drawn back, until I was touching her forearm. She snapped to attention and I smiled her way, trying to warm her heart with nothing but my face.

Our minds collided.

_My witch. My dearest, sweetest witch, the lady who gives my life meaning, how lovely your light shines! Why are we so far apart, my witch?_

_Fight the patterns, Haley._

_Why fight when they lead me to a lady so lovely? Surely the fates drive us toward a shared destiny!_

_Goddamnit, Haley. We practiced._

_How could I practice for this?_

Agonizing silence. I could feel Lady Estelle’s judgment weighing heavy on my chest, the tears welling up in my eyes. How could she be so cold to her familiar? How could she possibly pretend we weren’t bonded at the deepest levels, destined to live together in harmony until time loses the fight with entropy?

I felt her hand on the back of my neck, scratching just a touch. I purred, leaning in. Then she grabbed my scruff, hard, and jerked me back into reality.

Gone was the void, the smell of blossoms, the swirl of energy. We were back in the middle of Chicago, the sound of traffic coming in too loudly from an open window at the back of the building. I was the cat again, paws and all, and my darling Witch was busy putting out the candles.

“Get our patient settled in the recovery room,” my beautiful witch said. “Then come to my office. We need to talk.”

Thankfully Megan was out like a light so wheeling her out of the room was a cinch. I was right to tell her not to worry: Estelle did a great job. A lot of transmogrifiers working with pre-made components would advertise these unbelievable before and after pictures of their clients turning from linebackers to bombshells. Their secret? Not a one of them worried about the client being recognizable when the procedure was done. Megan looked much the same as she did when she came in the office: midwest-stocky, with broad shoulders and rail-straight hips, same skin tone and same mottled complexion. But when I reached down into her mana and dug around the whole of her life had changed.

But Megan was still… well, Megan. Couldn’t put my finger on how. I, on the other hand, was now the property of Lady Estelle.

With Megan put down I followed the smell of spicy, floral tea coming from Estelle’s office. When I stepped in she shoved warm teacup into my hands. “Drink,” she said. As I did she inspected the two piles of fur on top of my head like a mother worrying over a wound.

I felt the fizzy-bright energy of her magic surge through my brain and laughed.

"Sorry," I said. "Ticklish."

"Over sensitivity in cursed appendages is normal," she said, matter-of-factly. "You _swear_ you took every dose of that potion, right? This is important. This isn’t some kiddy cantrip you can just shrug off. And if you don't tamp these things down--"

"Yes, yes! You made that plenty clear. Don't worry; I got through it."

“The whole cup,” she said, pointing to the mug in my hands.

"Can I mix it next time? With bourbon, maybe. Or a nice can of tuna."

Estelle sat down to her tea, picked it up, and pointed it in my direction. "That's the curse talking, It'll keep digging its claws into your brain if you let it--"

"--claws." I let out a giggle, but Estelle wasn't having it. She slammed the desk, hard. I shrank away.

"You're too sensitive to the magic, Haley." She shook her head and put down her tea. “It’s not safe, dear.”

“Please, no. I'll be fine. I'm just... just coping with the new job duties, is all. Haven't done magic since my sophomore year at Salma. It'll come back."

"Right. You left for… for music, right?

"City College," I said, smiling. "They didn't offer a minor in magic, so."

The Lady stifled a laugh. Forget Juliard, forget the state schools, forget everything else: if you go to the only university known for churning out Witches and walk away with a music major from another college, you fucked up big time. And you need a job that pays because accompanying high school soloists sure-as-hell doesn't make rent, and Spotify cuts into your album profits when you're trying to make a living as a singer-songwriter at the piano. But being a witch's familiar sure did pay, long as you were aware of the consequences.

The tea cleared the haze in my brain. Comedown was a pain, though: my mind swam with humiliation as I remembered just how much I wanted to fulfill Lady Estelle's every wish.

“I’m sorry,” she said around a chuckle. “I shouldn’t laugh. Was it your grades? Just didn’t like the work?”

I looked into my mug and my eyes unfocused. “I don't know. Family stuff. Stuff just got to be too much, so I quit. It’s not important, anyway. I'm sorry. I just really need this job.”

"Saving up for a tour?”

I shrugged. "Maybe, yeah. I got an offer to open for Mana Crush.”

“Mana who?”

“They’re a magicore indie band. Two guitarists, bassist, drummer, and a lady who generates sounds from the Ley-lines under the concert venue. They’re on the rise - I think NPR brought them in for a concert a few weeks ago and now every venue in the region wants to book ‘em. And they pay, too; not just snacks and free beer.”

“Sounds fun.”

“My friend Jeremy plays bass and does vocals for them.”

Her eyebrow raised at that. “A guy fronting a magicore band?”

“I know. Weird, but they're getting bigger. That means agents, and promoters watching their openers— watching me, opening for them. It’s a big opportunity, Lady Estelle, and I need some savings before I take off."

“But you know, there is something nice about staying with you. Just… well…”

Estelle shook her head. "Okay. Okay. Just this once I'll look the other way. But you're taking a twelve pack of those poultices when you leave today. Take one a day, and if the parts are still there when you come back tomorrow I’m sending you right back home, okay?"

"Deal." I stood and offered a handshake. But as the blood rushed to my head I could feel the haze crusting back over my brain. When she went to take my paw I leaned forward, touched my cheek to hear hand, and rubbed my scent on her.

She pulled her hand away. "Haley, remember what I said about fighting the patterns."

I mewed. Mewed! I didn't want to at first but once I leaned into it it felt... right, somehow? Entirely appropriate for a girl with cat ears. Maybe the next session would give me a tail to flick as I did this song and dance.

"No, Haley. Stop. Breathe. Ground and center. Magic 101, remember? You are not my familiar; I am not your Witch. I am your boss, and I'm paying you to assist me in the performance of rituals."

"We turn boys into girls! Lady Estelle turns me into a cat!“ I added with a giggle. Estelle threw her hands up in defeat, clinched them into fists, and then wrapped them behind her head.

"Ok, familiar. Get me two sprigs of darkweed, a dash of maiden's milk, and a marigold. Crush it into a paste. Chop chop."

An order! I skipped over to a cabinet that had once served as a card catalog and picked each ingredient for her. I hummed my favorite song while I worked the components with a pestle until a dingy gray paste formed at the bottom of the bowl.

"Good. Now stuff it between your gums and cheeks."

"Beg your pardon?"

"I don't think that was a request," she said, eyes narrowing to a glare. So I shoved the awful mix into my mouth and tried not to gag at the taste.

The world slammed back into focus. I am not a cat. I am a musician trying to make rent while my career sputters to a stop. Estelle's paying me a small fortune to act as a familiar for a month - mostly hazard pay for taking on the downsides of the curses she applies to her patients.

"Like smelling salts for a curse," Estelle said by way of explanation. "We're done for today. I'm canceling our other appointments, and you're going home to rest."

"I can get better at this," I said, voice wavering. "I'm just rusty."

"You better," Estelle said. "Or you're going to get stuck like this forever."

I was sent home with a quarter of my pay plus a generous tip from Megan. A quarter of my pay was still enough to clear rent and eat for a month, so at least the tour would be covered.

Home was a small townhouse in the suburbs of Chicago. It was hell on the commute, but rent was low enough that I could spring for a three-bed and turn the unused one into a studio. Jeremy and I shared the place as a home studio - me for my EPs and he for demo tapes for Mana Clash. There was no aesthetic past messiness - instruments hanging from walls, studio computer on one wall, egg-crate lined walls by a microphone on a stand, and in one corner a stool without an instrument. My corner, actually.

It was covered in a thick pile of wadded-up sheet music dotting the ground. It... well, it hasn't been a good year for my next album, that's for sure.

I walked into the house to hear Jeremy practicing the thundering drumbeats of "Buy Tomorrow." Most gigs Mana Clash played used it as a set opener: rock beats, fat chords, the kind of "shut up and fucking listen to the band band" song that sometimes, when the stars aligned, got the audience on their side for the weird prog-ish stuff they hid later on in the set.

It was a stupid fun song to play, especially for a crowd. I should know - I’ve ringed in for a few shows here and there, mostly playing the Voidcaster, and you couldn't _not_ smile when jamming out to the song.

But when I walked into the room the music went silent and he turned to stare at me. It was in those moments that Jeremy reminded me of the worst of my father: the vapid stares, judging eyes, and silence - complete, not-a-peep silence that made you want to fill the void with something, anything to get things moving again.

"Can I help you?" I said with a nervous chuckle.

"Ears," he managed to get out, and then yelped as my tail snaked around my body and into view. "Cat. Parts."

"Yeah?" I laughed at him. "Hadn't noticed. You do realize that, like, half of all teenage girls have animal parts nowadays? They sell six packs of the potions at Broomsticks for ten bucks, and they get passed around the city schools like candy."

"I know. Just, well, never took you for the modification type is all. More of a 'take pride in your body' than a fad chaser."

"It's a perk of the new job," I said, shrugging. "Besides, 'pride in my body' is something I'm working on."

I slid past his drum set, twirled, and slid into my chair. Yeah. I twirled. Sue me for liking my new tail. With a short hiss of breath I focused my energy into my fingers, put them to the wall, and unzipped reality by about two feet. A swirl of prismatic energy belched into the room. I reached into the hole in the world, felt around, and pulled back a keyboard manifested from my will alone.

I made the thing back in Salma for a senior project. The profs were so furious with me - apparently hiding a jet black keyboard in the seams of my reality tended to mess up the work of the Witch majors in the next building over. But here, hidden away in the recesses of my own home, nobody gave a fuck, so that’s something. I played a quick arpeggio and then leaned on the keys, hard, until mush came out. Then I laid my head in my arms and groaned.

"That's not usually your style either."

"It's the job," I said into the keys. "I'm doing familiar work for Lady Estelle."

"The gender-swap queen?" He laughed. "Never mind what I said earlier. This sounds like it's right up your alley."

I pushed back from the keys and started noodling. A waltz, complete with the oom-pah-pah in the left hand and a plodding little melody line in the right. "At least she's doing medicine," I said by way of apology. You should have seen the girl we worked on today. Linebacker when she came in - I think she played college ball - and by the time my Witch was done with her she was... just wow, man. Not going to win any modeling contests anytime soon, but you should have seen the look in her eyes. The joy. The..."

"Your Witch." His eyes flickered and a smile crossed his face.

"Yes!" My ears flicked to and fro and my tail flagged high above my head. "My witch. She... well, she uses me, for lack of a better word, to focus the right energies into the patient. It's sort of like she's the light, and I'm the lens--"

"I know," he interrupted me. "My mom did familiar work in the months after Day One. Tore her apart."

"It's not exactly a spa day, sure. But it's work and it pays well. And besides, this isn't forever! Just until I get signed."

He chuckled at that.

"We can make this work, Jeremy. You with Mana Clash, me with my act. We just need the cards to fall our way."

He closed his eyes. Nodded. Then stood from his stool, sticks in hand, and pointed to my ears. "We could make this job work for us too. A little maid dress, collar with a bell, little ribbons on the ears? Yeah. We could do a side band duo thing. I could wear a button-up and a bow tie and we could do a whole Victorian vibe with our stage presentation--"

I grabbed a wadded-up piece of sheet and threw it at him. He started playing the beat to "Cat Scratch Fever" and only stopped when I reached for a microphone stand.

"But seriously, you can be a fucking witch. Why are you slummin' it with us non-magical folks?"

"I'm not going to be a real witch, Jeremy. I did my first-year exams and barely passed. A magic-user of my talent can build spell components in a factory, maybe make some potions in a fulfillment center. But all that stuff's produced in India now. Hijara there make it on the cheap."

"Still. You can make good money with boutique witching. You could start a shop like Broomsticks: stock all the stuff from India but add your own twist, you know? And Lady Estelle charges - what, ten grand to make a man a woman? - and I'm sure you're getting a decent cut of that."

I shrugged. "It's a living. I'll probably cover my expenses by the end of the week."

"Then you make more in a week than I do as a studio musician in a month, and you don't have to play commercial jingles on demand." He laughed.

"I mean, sure. I could make more money as a Witch. But you know what?"

"You didn't want that," he said in a sing-song voice.

"I didn't want that! I wanted to be a musician! I wanted... I wanted the applause, the music, signing albums in the hall, the whole nine. I wanted to share my gift with the world. Make my own way, instead of trying to do what made my father proud."

There was silence there. Then it gave way to a ringing in my ear, then the faint sound of his heartbeat against his rib cage. That was new, I thought, but it wasn't altogether a bad thing. If I closed my eyes I could hear the din of the city outside my window: a backhoe digging out pulverized asphalt, power tools on the high rise next door, the clink of a mason's trowel fixing the tuck-pointing on a library nearby. I could get used to this: a morning with Estelle, afternoons laying around in the sun, an evening studying for my second years--

"So you left Witching school because you failed out."

"Yes."

“And you play a piano that you tuck away in a pocket just outside of reality.”

“Yes.”

“That you created from scratch using just the power of your brain.”

“You got it.”

He shook his head. “You’re a terrible liar, Haley. But now you’re a terrible liar on our tour. Congratulations, I guess?”

When I woke up the next day things were only getting worse: cat muzzle, claws for nails, and in my head I kept seeing Lady Estelle smiling at me, almost as if on repeat, the image filling me with intense joy.

I came into work the next day wearing a hat, a big dress, and a pair of tights that kept what remained of my tail clamped firmly against my leg. I made myself busy replacing candles and components in the ritual room until Estelle made it into the office.

I couldn’t meet her eyes when she walked into the room with me. I was replacing a large black candle at the head of the table and sprinkling it with fur I had gathered from my ears last week, as Witches had done for this spell for the past four centuries. And then I saw her, the lady I had prostrated myself to yesterday, the lady who had in the span of moments gone from an employer to a god, and I just couldn’t bring myself to look at her.

“You took the poultices, I take it.”

I nodded, still not meeting her eyes.

She took a breath. “It’s okay. Really. I just didn’t expect things to take hold so quickly. With most familiars I get a handful of sessions before I have to have this conversation. And... well, I may have snapped a bit. Let's have a look at you."

She reached out to touch my temples and I shied away.

“I’m not going to hurt you!”

I laughed at that, put down the candle, and took a seat on the ritual table. “It’s just, well.”

I took off the hat and my ears sprang to life. A compulsion came over me: flick the ear with a finger, shake the kinks out with a shiver. To her credit Estelle didn’t say a word.

“I lost myself yesterday. One second this was a summer job that’d leave me looking like some anime fetish given life, and the next there was this... this clarity to my world.”

Estelle nodded toward the table; I scooted over so that she could join me.

“I want to give you the world, Lady Estelle. Like the divine world had opened up and lit up the path to my destiny. All I wanted to be, all I wanted of life? It was all you. All clear. All calm.

“It would have been so easy, Estelle. All you had to do was let me give in.”

She put her hands over mine. “Some witches might have done just that, Haley. There’s a reason this job’s got a reputation. You’ve got Witches out there that recruit dropouts like you, turn ‘em, and then get them to sign away everything. Law hasn’t caught up to us yet - how can it catch up when you can rewrite someone’s brain into giving enthusiastic consent?”

“But that’s not your style.”

She nodded and squeezed my hand. I felt her energy flow into my body, warm and soothing. It sought out the changes from yesterday’s ritual and lit them up in my mind - bits of brain, ears, tails, the paw pads that felt like calluses on my hands. I expected something from her then - a reversal spell, perhaps, or just a flat-out sprint to change me completely - but nothing came.

Instead she dug deeper. Down, past my conscious mind and my defenses. Down, into the place from which music came, staccato drumbeats melding with stride piano licks, the speakeasy that was my Citadel.

And then, as quickly as I she took my hand in hers, we were both just there, lost in a tiny speakeasy tucked away in my head. Me, plucking out “everybody wants to be a cat” on the piano; and her, pouring a gin and tonic for herself behind the bar. When I looked to my hands I saw they were still these large, puffy paws, complete with jellybean-pink pads, and immediately started missing notes on the piano.

“No wonder it worked so well,” Estelle said, her voice echoing through the empty expanse of my mind. “You took 100-level Citidels but didn’t go on to Defensive Magic. And by the look of things, well.”

The doors to the bar flew open. A dozen  cat-people flooded into the bar, bartenders pouring Manhattans in stemware, a couple of calicos in flapper dresses dancing a Charleston, their tails swaying in perfect synchronization. The crowd crescendoed into endless conversation, cheers, some even singing along with me.

It was... well, new, to say the least. Usually when I came here you’d find a few magical folks, maybe some Fae, a few wisps dotting the walls. But cats? Cats were an entirely new thing.

“You didn’t put this place in mothballs when you quit the Witching track, did you?”

I felt my tail droop.

“Don’t get me wrong, Haley. This is fantastic work, all things considered. This isn’t your first rodeo. I mean, I’ve seen the Citadels of Witches who gave up their craft. They’re musty, with drafts under the windows and the stench of mold wafting from the cellar. This is something else entirely.

“I come here to relax,” I said. One of the cats brought me a white russian - I played a few bars on the left hand to free the right for a drink. “Kept it around so that I could practice in my dreams. You know. Spirit walks and all that junk.”

“Spirit walks? That’s not exactly first-year material.”

“I read a few blogs.”

“Show me what you can do,” Estelle said, waving me toward the dance floor. The cats found their way to tables. I hesitated at first but then the Witch shoved me out into the open and sat down at the piano.

She started to play some blues.

“Didn’t know you played.”

“I read a few blogs,” she replied, sticking her tongue out. “Whenever you’re ready.”

I froze.

“Go on now!”

I breathed. Grounded. Centered. Flexed my wrists. I imagined three hundred milliliters of water forming into a small sphere at the tips of my fingers but all I got was dribbles of water leaking onto the floor. I imagined a thin lick of flame sprouting from my thumb but got a burn. Earth put a grass stain on my fur. Air, a Marilyn Monroe moment with a skirt I hadn’t entirely been sure I was wearing.

“Forgot your forms?”

I nodded. “It's been a bit.”

“You don’t need to remember,” she said, and then laughed. “Just see where your curiosity takes you.”

“That's your one cat joke for the day.” I shook out the stiffness in my limbs, touched the ground, and stretched, cat-like, until my body felt ready. Then I stood in the ready stance I’d learned from Professor Belfast’s Spellcaster Essentials class: knees bent, arm extended, eyes focused on the tips of fingers that spread out like a pianist waiting for a conductor’s count-in. I manifested a wisp of light on my index finger and watched it dance around my wrist like a fly drunk on fermented fruit.

"You're not cut out for this stance,” Estelle told her.

"I know," she said. "I'm a pianist."

“Then stop trying to cast spells and just let your body play.”

The trill of a tango welled up around me. Melodicas laid a carpet for me to dance on; violins wailed backdrops of ecstasy and despair; a cat at the bar produced castanets and clacked out a rhythm that my body rode, side to side, lost in the music.

My eyelids drooped and I dipped backwards, arms over my head and tracing long, lazy arcs on the floor. The wisp grew to a bright light; bright light to raw energy, and soon the dance floor reached up to embrace me with sensuous, angel-white strands of light.

I could see Estelle, hazy through a veil of energy, smiling wide. The floor pulled me upright again and I began to stomp to the rhythm. My eyes fixed on hers. I reached to her, my claw brushing against her arm, but when I went to pull her into the dance she pulled her hand away. A voyeur, watching the cat dance.

She flashed me a wan smile and a wink that said "Continue, Lady-in-training."

I rolled my eyes and fell back into the rhythm of my spell. Lust, my fingers tracing up my arm, over the shoulder and down my side, catching Estelle’s gaze over my shoulder. Light fell over me like rain. I began to sing: humming at first, until the melody solidified on my tongue and found its way into broken phrases of scat, humming, the occasional lyric.

“I can’t love you as woman,” I sang, the melody a husky alto. I pictured my body as it was a week ago and the light enveloped me in warmth, pressure. “I can’t love you as a cat. But I can love you as music, as rhythm, 'cause that—”

The light shattered into shards that bounced from my body. Paws replaced with hands; fur with milky-smooth skin; and the tail fell from my back, flailing in time to the castanets’ manic rhythm, until the light rose up to drag it away. The song reached a crescendo and I began to spin around the floor, arms tucked in to my sides, until I was feet away from Estelle.

On the final chord the world snapped back into focus. We were back in the ritual room, Estelle sitting on the ritual table and I striking a final pose, human as the day I was born. She was smiling at me - a big, beaming sort of smile that reeked of both pride and a strong sense of “I told you so.”

She reached up to put a hand on my head and flicked at what remained of a cat ear. “You missed a spot.”

I was too busy marveling at what had changed to pay her any mind. I came in this morning looking like something out of the bowels of some internet subculture, and now I was just here, visibly myself, as if nothing had changed.

“But I didn’t follow any spell, Estelle. Aren’t there supposed to be protocols or something?”

“Usually, yes. That’s how we teach it at Salma anyway. Your method was unorthodox but not new: it reminded me of the Roma Danceweavers from Estonia. They would dance to apply hexes to their enemies and charms to their friends, guided by the fires at their feet and the rhythm of a drum from the caravan.

“...but that’s some 200 level stuff in Salma. It’s okay if you didn’t know. But what isn’t okay is this; why did you quit, Haley?”

“I just did. That’s all.”

“Bullshit.”

“I wasn’t a good witch.”

“I’d say a girl who could undo a curse that’s been around since, I don’t know, the early 1700s can’t be quantified as ‘bad.’ You had talent, Haley.”

“You don’t know.”

“I saw your admissions test,” Estelle said, and almost instantly regretted it. I could see her shy away from me, eyes closed, hands wringing, shame washing over her body. “I taught you! When you said you wanted the job I jumped at the opportunity. You were loved, Haley. Respected.”

I laughed.

“So again, why did you quit? Was it your father? Was it Admissions Day?”

“I’m a pianist," I replied with a laugh, and Estelle left well enough alone.

Mana Clash played The Place Between that night. Not as part of the tour - at least not officially - but as a final sound check on their set before boarding the tour bus. They let me play piano for a few of their deep cuts that night; Jeremy talked the band into it, said it’d help me get in the pocket when the tour started up in earnest.

A few groupies surged from their chairs and hugged tight to the stage when we walked on. They whistled - loud, too loud, did they not see the fucking cat ears on my head? But I tried to hide the wince behind the flowing sleeves of my dress, cut like it came out of a late aughts fantasy film. Rita and Mona, the guitarists slash Witches of the outfit, painted a celestial landscape over the rafters and roof. Snow fell - warm snow that dissipated without water and left the faint smell of burned oak on your nose - and snowshoe hares began to dart under empty tables and chairs all around. Mana Clash played their winter cycle - three songs about teenage love, loss, and dropping out of college - and I did my best to fill out the soundscape with fat, vamping chords.

“I’m just a pianist,” I mouthed to myself, laughing again. Damn good pianist, sure, but it wasn’t like I was the next Hiromi, or the next Corea. Meddling at best, average at worst. Garden variety.

I ran the worst through my mind, over and over, in rhythm to the winter cycle. “I’m a pianist.” I could have heard her out. She'd brought me on, taught me, _set me up with a gender confirming spellcaster_ when I asked - but no. “I’m a pianist,” says I, and then I excused myself to prep for this show. "I know how that feels. Your daughter does, too."

"Right." He laughed and ran a hand through his scraggly, salt-and-pepper hair. "Preaching to the choir."

It was about at that moment that Aria came through a side door of the boudoir. I could see past her to what remained of her own Citadel: dusty, dark, the must of misuse and neglect wafting in like a bad fart. "Rusty," was all she offered as an explanation.

"Glad you could make it, Aria. Can you pull down the polymorph poultices for me? I assume you still know your way around."

She tiptoed through the room but never took her eyes off mine, running her fingers over the racks of potions that ran the length of the room. There were at last a hundred different bottles, some stashed in a closet closed with red velour curtains, and as she made her way around she'd pluck a bottle here or there to put on the vanity. Fish-shaped bottles, just like she had at the bar, only mine were hand-labelled and lacked the glowing powder most batch-produced poultices mixed in. "Gosh, I haven't been in your Citadel since we fooled around our sophomore year. So precise. So _regimented_! My auntie back home taught me like this. Dance is true; dance is passionate; dance knows its friends from its enemies."

She put bottles on the vanity and shot another nasty glare in Zeke's direction. Zeke shuffled in his seat, uncomfortable, so I closed the gap between us and put an arm on his shoulder. "Whenever you're ready, Zeke, take a sip from any bottle you'd like. Nothing here's permanent, and if you end up not liking the look I can wipe it all away. Once we find one you like I'll build out the ritual with Lady Aria here."

"The hell I will."

"Please. I could use the help and it will do you good." I kept eye contact with Zeke; I was losing him in all this back and forth. The smile that had dotted his face gave way to wistfulness, sadness. I nodded to him by way of encouragement and he knocked back one of the bottles.

His body took to the changes quite well, all told. His legs clapped together and then disappeared under a layer of orange-white scales that wrapped around him like tape around a pole. Slits formed on his torso that began to pulse with every breath. The salt-and-pepper hair on his head fell to the ground as a crown of guidefins crossed over his skull, ending at the temples.

He laughed, patted himself down to feel the new body, but this wasn't a new sensation for him. You can tell when someone experiences transmutation magic for the first time; there's nervous laughter, or overexcited glee, or the spark of experimentation with new parts. But Zeke showed none of this. Even as he drank potions that gave him the fish-tail of his dreams, filled his head with memories of a wonderful life under the sea, made him ten years younger, there was a deep sadness there.

I leaned in close to him so we could have a private chat. "Look, I know that Aria can be a little... well, _touchy_ at times. I'll talk to her about it. But if you're not comfortable with her here, just say the word and I'll see her out. This is your dream, Zeke. Your chance to be the thing you always knew in your heart was your true self. We're going to make some big decisions over the next few days. Body types, contracts, last will and testament, everything.

"You're about to leave your life behind forever; you don't get to come back from the spell I'll use. I just want you to feel comfortable while you're doing it, okay?"

He looked past me to Aria, who sulked in a corner of the boudoir, eyes narrowed, as if trying to bore a hole through his skull with just her gaze alone. "It's okay," he began, and stopped. Zeke turned his new torso in the chair to face her, now covered in irridescent orange scales and sunfish-like fins, and cleared his throat.

"It's okay," he said, stronger now. "I understand that you're angry. You... you knew Hallan--"

Aria rolled her eyes. "Her name is Haley, newfin. You'd be wise to remember it."

"Right." He folded his hands in what used to be his lap, twiddled his thumbs, and searched for words. "I'm sorry. I only get it right about half the time. How is he?  ...She, I mean. Haven't heard from her in years now. Not that I'd blame her if she deleted the contacts altogether. Not sure if I'd keep my number on hand, after what I did. But it's... that's in the past now, ma'am. I can't change what I did then. And I don't feel like I can mend the fences that I broke. There's, god, a decade separating her and me now. Wounds like that, they scar over. We get on with our lives."

"How convenient for you. Do you know how many nights I stayed up with her? Cried with her? Talked her down from panic attacks from something as stupid as _ironing a shirt_ because you demanded that kind of perfection, day in, day out?"

My feet grew roots and my voice hanged in my throat, dead to any ability to stop this. She encircled him with the sound of her voice, the sheer _presence_ of ten years' rage given an outlet. Fingers pointing like daggers; eyes bloodshot, face taut like a bowstring.

"You ruined her." Aria spat. "She hated herself because of you. Hated having the audacity of being _born a certain way_. Other kids spent their days worrying about prom, and boys, and bands; she got to spend hers hiding deep in the closet, scrutinizing _every little thing_ she did just in case you picked up her trail."

He wanted to respond. Desperately so, even; I could see tears forming in the corners of his eyes, the collapse of his frame as Aria came closer and closer, her voice ringing in his ears. But she wouldn't relent. She _never_ relented, given a chance to air out her grievances.

I clasped my hands together, squeezed until the knuckles went white.

"And above it all there was that intense fear. Knowing that if you caught even the slightest whiff that your darling son would turn out to be a Witch you'd have shipped her off to a camp. That was her reality. _That_ is what you did to your daughter."

"I was harsh," Zeke said. He didn't try to look at her. "I was worse than harsh. I taught her to be afraid of me and I taught her to hate her own body, and I told myself I was doing it to save her. And then she was gone and I had years to do nothing but sit with what I'd done."

He raised his eyes. They were wet.

"I'm not asking for a happy ending. I came here because I can't stand being in the body that hurt her. That's the only honest thing I have left."

Aria stared at him. Her mouth worked but nothing came out. The rage that had carried her around the room — ten years of it, sharp and ready — caught on something it didn't expect to find.

She turned away. "Don't think this means I forgive you."

"I would never," Zeke said.

That was when his new center of gravity gave out. The fishy tail slid off the seat without warning and he went down — not slapstick, just sudden, an old man's body that no longer obeyed him. He hit the floor hard. The chair clattered into the mirror behind him. Glass cracked; one of Aria's bottles tipped and let its potion bleed out across the velour. Zeke didn't try to get up. He lay there on his side, scales catching the candlelight, breathing too fast.

Aria's face was a mess of things. She didn't laugh. Whatever she'd been holding onto had gone out from under her at the same moment Zeke had gone down.

I cracked open my escape hatch. It was a small stick of driftwood that cost at least a month's expenses for the van. A friend had made a set for me a few months back; driftwood for merfolk, balsa for sky pixies, maple for Salma-- one for each colony I had ever worked with.

I snapped it, touched it to the floor, and screamed: "Atlantis, Urchin Inn."

The world turned inside out and for just a moment we hanged above the Divine Loom below, its energies burning bright in the space below our bodies. Then, a powerful suction, at the toes, the hips, until we swirled into ourselves and out into the open oceans of Atlantis.

We landed in a small small air bubble protected from the sea by a giant dome. Jutting into the center of the dome was a long, smoothed slate bar with stools, complete with a tiny airlock the barkeep could use to serve drinks. They'd originally intended for this to be a scuba bar for the locals but the concept didn't take off. They ended up selling the space as a port place for us Witches, but the price was far from cheap. (Tourist trap, remember?)

Our bodies came into themselves with a great, sucking sound. Bubbles of air, displaced by the volume of our bodies violently invading their space, escaped in a cloudy, fizzy haze. A group of mermen in the middle of a game of underwater darts stopped to stare, and when they saw Aria they gave a little cheer.

Zeke didn't need to be told twice. In a single, graceful slide he found his way to the deep end of our little pool and wriggled out into the open ocean of the bar to cheers and raised glasses. Flounder waved to me from the glass, shaker in hand, and pointed to the bits of glass and potion stains that splattered my dress.

"Bad consult, mama?"

I didn't answer him. "Get this man a drink on my tab. Can you keep your eyes on him? I have some things to clean up here."

Our bodies may have never left the Inn but I snapped Aria and myself back to my boudoir. Glass crunched underfoot; I set my broom to the task of cleaning up the glass and poultice leavings. Aria and I slipped into the Citadel beside mine: a simple place, musty with the smell of neglect, where not even the power worked on the first try. We stared at our dim outlines through a barre-lined mirror, both lost in hazes of memories: late night cram sessions for divination; evocation practice; watching Aria dance with a broom until it came to life, jubilant and excited, to sweep up the mess we'd made with pizza crumbs in the deepest recesses of her own mind.

Aria laughed and rummaged through a junk pile near the door. "I haven't been here since I moved to Atlantis." She pulled out a candle, lit it, and went over to to a large, above-ground tub of water near the back of the studio. Off came the cover, and with a whisper and a wave of her hand the water set about cleaning itself.

"Last addition," she said. "Not sure if you ever saw it. You were off having adventures while I was putting Haley back together, so forgive me. My memory's hazy."

Aria slid into the tiny wave pool. As her skin touched the water it melted back into the irridescent, green fins that she wore back at the shop. She must have spent months on this little bath, perfecting every little detail, for the shift to be so instant. Gauging by how barren the dance studio looked, save for that pool, that was intentional. Her Citadel wasn't a workspace, per se; it was a reminder. A single, focused statement of purpose: _I renounce this life._

"I'm so mad that I ended up by you in the Loom. Had I known you'd share space in my head for the rest of my life I'd have just faked being sick that day."

"And I liked you more when you were just playing the sushi chef." I chuckled. "Was kind of hoping I'd get more of that."

She slid deeper into the tub and her tail flopped over the edge, emerald-green and shining. She stretched her arms up and over the tub's back, yawned, cracked her neck. Relaxed, real, and always, always reading: a drag queen's happy place, and Aria was more than happy open the library for me.

"You're the one who came begging for seconds, hunty. I _left_. New city, new body, new _everything_. I was _happy_. Men wanted me. Women, too. And there were no... no questions. 'Can you make my house clean itself?', 'Can you show me my true love?' No asking for old pictures, no talking about my power to partners, nothing. Just me, Aria Extravaganza, the baddest bitch to walk Atlantis's walkway.

"And then you come calling, asking me a favor like some lovesick puppy, and I believed you. _I promise he'll be good._ _I swear he can keep a secret._ You _knew_ who you were sending. You _knew_."

More memories, a flood of memories: the smell of her hair, the ticklish spot at the crease of her butt, the stretch of her lips as we kissed, lost in the Loom and in each other.

She slid her arm out of the water and made sure to brush against my hand. Electricity and lust and sour grapes rippled through the water between us.

"But that's not what I do."

"Just pointing out that you don't _need_ the money. So... what is it? This another one of your causes, boo? Dumping a pile of slats in front of a friend so they can mend a fence?"

I shook my head and laughed. "Zeke wants to be half-fish and you're the only place in the nation that could house him. That's all it is."

She furrowed her brow.

"Okay, okay. A little fence-mending. Nobody else is going to help him; might as well be me."

"Who says we need to mend these fences? He _abused_ Haley. Don't give him the pleasure. You know Gertrude did this to make him suffer. Maybe... maybe he should suffer. Now and for the rest of his miserable life."

I took a lap around her Citadel. This wasn't the first time I'd taken this walk, mind you. There was the time she slept with Haley, drunk on too much wine and the bone-deep conviction that they would end up together forever. She rolled around in the tank, moaning; partly in boredom, partly to make me feel guilty about giving a crap about her life and her choices. She raised an arm out of the tub and dismissed me with a wave of her hand. "Unimportant. You're just changing the subject. Zeke abused her daughter. He deserves retribution. Nothing in that warrants a conversation about our power."

"But that's _exactly_ why it does," I fired back. "People like Zeke, they're never going to find the power we have. He gave his link to it up when he left Haley for dead, back when she came to Salma. "Our people could make gold if we wanted to." I waltzed around her tub, fingers tracing along the rim. Her eyes followed me as I walked. She laughed. "Even when they hurt you."

"Especially when they hurt you."

"But you should have invited Haley at the very least. It's her father. Her wound to heal. But no, you're just going to give her father a pass because... what, because you want to be the bigger person? That's not your place."

"Someone has to do it."

"It should be Haley. But don't worry; she's already on her way."

The Citadel shook with three solid knocks and I found myself flying up, up out of the room and back into the bar. Across from me Aria sat motionless, a sneer on her face and her knuckles white with tension. Flounder waved to get my attention and pointed to a stiff drink in his hand. Outside the dome Zeke was flying around the room, flippers going crazy as he laughed these huge, belly laughs that reverberated through our little air pocket.

A prank, pulled by college friends who wanted a chance to get her out of her day-to-day doldrum. But Zeke's smile told all, and Haley didn't entirely seem sure how to respond.

Flounder put the drink in the airlock. "Sure I can't get you ladies a round? I know when you fightin'."

I laughed, shrugged. Aria came back into herself and slipped back into the water, tail and all, to the raucous cheers of the boys in the Urchin Inn.

"I got a text," Haley said, her eyes still transfixed on her father. "Said I should come to Atlantis as soon as I could. And now I'm here and my father is... is _this._"

She pointed to the merman outside the tub, who was too busy being taught a dance to notice his own daughter was there, staring.

What was I supposed to do? Walk her out to talk to her father? _Oh hey, remember the girl Aria accused you of ruining? Surprise family reunion!_ I made a mental note to never ask a favor of Aria again and put a hand on her shoulder.

"I should have called sooner. I'm sorry if seeing your father like this makes you angry."

"Confused, mostly. And full of... odd feelings, to be quite honest. Feelings I'm not comfortable going into here. Can we get him back on the surface to talk?"

It was about at that point that Aria came back into her limp body, arms stretched and her body returned to the mermaid the area knew best. How about you two wait him out at Carmen's van? He has such _exciting news_ to share about his future; maybe she can fill you in back on the surface it there, if she's okay with it. You _are_ okay with it; right, Carmen?"

I shook my head and willed my body to breathe water. "We'll figure something out," I said, shrugging, and ducked out under the moon pool to take the long, shameful swim back to my home.

Zeke came back to shore around sunset. Aria wasn't in tow, thank the Divine; Last thing I needed was her stirring the pot with Haley and her father.

His fins melted away, scale by scale, and as they did his legs found purchase on the sand below. The poultice and hours of swimming left his legs weak; I reached out to catch him before he fell straight on his face. "Everything okay?" he said. I handed him a towel to cover up. Framed in dying, orange-red light that blazed from the horizon he looked... frailer than I remember.

"Better now. I can't apologize enough for what she just did to you. I'll comp your estate the consult costs."

"It's okay. She's right, you know." He looked to the ground and wrapped a hand around my shoulder for support. "I did keep her locked up for the longest time. Chided her when she stood like a girl, grounded her when I found her clothes. Anything -- anything to keep my darling boy safe."

He chuckled to himself, shook his head, let his eyes close. "I bet she's lighting the world on fire now. She was always a smart cookie, even if she was so reserved. Way I figure it, after I stopped trying to clip her wings she _soared_.

We made our way through the loose sand, onto a bridge over dune grass, and into the parking lot. Patrons at a little souvenir shop across the street stopped to stare at us: a sopping wet white girl in a black dress walking someone's grandfather to a windowless panel van, our eyes locked in thousand-yard stares.

"I remember, back when Haley was at Salma, she'd send me these... these messages. Once a week, every week, I'd get a call and an email. "I went back and read them all before I came to you. She was taking Girl Juice, I remember. Face changing, voice changing. She looked like a goddamn stranger. I ignored all of them - too painful to see, you know? - and... well... Can't believe how much I missed."

I opened the van's back door for him. "You should tell her that, you know. Before it's too late. I can't guarantee you'll keep those memories after this is all over. This ain't an exact science."

"I read the wavier. You don't have to keep reminding me." He shut the door behind him. I started pacing.

"Just making sure, is all. Bathroom is behind the sliding door on the passenger side. It's in a little pocket universe, though, so you'll want to keep the door cracked for oxygen. Clean up and I'll start working on paperwork."

While he showered I took a few minutes to set up my makeshift patio: pull the cloth canopy from the top of the van, folding table and chairs stored behind the driver's seat, citronella candles, and a string of LED lights that I'd enchanted to snake around the patio on my command. A snap of my fingers turned on the van's stereo; a beckoning motion set my little wet bar to mixing up a Manhattan. From there it was just me, the setting sun, and a fistful of dry contract copy that needed blanks filled in.

I lost myself in it all: the drink, the gentle seaside breeze, the scratch of my pen against paper. It wasn't until Haley came back, bucket of chicken in hand, that I even noticed time passing.

Haley stood about five feet nothing. Her pale white skin popped against a black shift dress and a leather messanger bag slung over one shoulder. "Uh huh."

"You okay?"

"I saw him when you two walked up from the beach. He's old."

You and me both, crying in the dorm room after we had our first shot of Girl Juice, taking stock of what we lost to get there. Don't try to pull your nonchalant bullshit on me."

That got my attention. I put down the contract; took a breath, long and slow; and leaned back into my chair, hands behind my head, letting my nostrils fill with the fresh seaside air. "You didn't think to call me before now."

"He's my client, Haley. And in my view, when people struggle you have a duty to reduce that suffering. And after this, he's going to be a new person. Mind wiped, body changed. Back to a happy, functioning member of society -- just like you and Estelle do, to be honest."

She scoffed at me. "This is the guy who'd rummage through my drawers looking for dresses. Shout at me if I walked to the girl's toy aisle. _Beat me_ if I stood like a girl in public, let alone be anything less than perfectly manly. After all that, you'd still help him?"

"Even then."

She opened her mouth to respond but was interrupted by the sound of my van's back door opening. Zeke - her father, the man who had both raised her and left her for dead on Salma's doorstep - stood there, dressed in an undershirt and a pair of slacks, his jaw agape at the sight of his daughter.

"You look..." Zeke began. He cleared his throat; adjusted his shirt; looked to the ground, the sky, the beach, anywhere but in the direction of her daughter.

"You look well," he finished, and in a daze walked over to the table. "It's good to see you again, Haley."

You'd think ten years separating father and daughter would trigger an emotional reunion - hugs, claps on the shoulder, a hearty "how have you been?" to really set things off - but not here. Under the buzzing electric lights of a beachside parking lot, for the first time in ten years, Haley had a chance to reconnect with her father, but he wasn't there. Physically, sure; the man standing before her had all the same blood, same bones, same voice. But they belonged to a stranger, lost to time and the scabbing of old wounds.

Zeke struggled with words. "It's nice to see you. Surprising, too; I thought this would be confidential."

I shrugged. "We are Sisters in the Loom. Not exactly easy to hide."

"That's okay." he walked toward Haley and as he did she shot straight out of her chair. Shoulders stiff, eyes wide with something between fright and excitement, she planted her feet as her father took her shoulders in her arms. "I still see my boy in there, but I'm sure that's just me. "Still a girl, still hustling. Tried making it in a band. Quit. Got a job with a lady who turns boys into girls. Made some mistakes along the way."

She nodded up toward the cat ears on her head and sneered.

"I wasn't going to say anything. Figured it was just fashion; you always liked those funny animal cartoons. You begged me to get you a cat costume when you younger. Remember that? I put my foot down because, well, it's not exactly the _manliest_ choice you could have made, but you were just so insistent on ears and a tail."

Her face went blank.

"Come on. Don't you remember that? I grounded you for a whole week." He squeezed her shoulders and begged with raised eyebrows, incredulous frown on his face.

"I don't remember much from then. Some things, sure; cartoons, toys, field trips. But when I think of home there's just... it's like a picture that won't come into focus."

Zeke's body deflated; proud, "that's-my-kid!" shoulders drooping; "maybe-we-can-start-again" eyes closing; the swagger releasing its grip on Haley's shoulders, his arms swinging at his sides.

"I see," he managed, looking to the ground.

Haley scrunched her shoulders. _What the fuck did you expect?_ her body seemed to shout. _You can never go home again, especially if you kicked someone out on their ass._ I could see the words forming on her tongue; words she'd said a hundred times before, too drunk on cheap wine, reading the occasional, "pretend nothing is wrong" email from her father, asking for a recipe or help remembering an old movie. In the end, none of this came out. What have been the point of talking as their two ships passed in the night?

"It's probably better this way. Nothing to remember, nothing to forgive. Just a nasty past left where it belongs and my father made a stranger by the ravages of time and neglect."

She turned to me. "I'll be in my Citadel working on the ritual. Let's just get this over with."

The history of magic contains no shortage of hexes and curses that disappear people from polite society. The Brujas of Spanish-controlled Florida perfected no less than five different marine curses, all meant to banish inconvenient onlookers to the surf, never to return. Two-spirit tribesfolk of Northern Michigan forests would turn aggressive traders into the hawks that protected their grain. All a Witch like me had to do was tweak the ritual to fit the requests of the client and unleash the least effective curse in the history of Witchcraft: instead of deterring the unwilling, I made dreams come true and built a referral stream while I was at it.

Having Haley and Aria along for the ride was just a bonus. Working this out on my own would take weeks; weeks for which Zeke would need to find lodgings, and weeks for which I wouldn't be paid. Aria needed to be there, sure, if she wanted a cut of Zeke's estate to go to Atlantis's coffers; and Haley came along for the feeling of closure. But it was nice to have the band back together again, even if we were working in an awkward, tense silence.

We worked out of Haley's Citadel that evening. For starters, she was one door down from my own Citadel for components and such. She had more room than Aria or I had in our rooms; she'd opted for a wide speakeasy with a piano and a dance floor. More than anything it felt _right_ to solve the problem of her father.

Aria and I huddled around a round table, weaving energy into a candle and a long frond of seaweed. Haley, meanwhile, sat at the piano plunking out a melody, paper and pencil on the music stand. We worked on the components in silence while time lost its meaning; outside this speakeasy we huddled in the fan, foreheads touching and bodies slack, while Zeke sat on the patio eating his last meal as a human being.

A melody took shape on the air, jazzy and a little funky. Then: a sour note, a rest, and the cacophony of two hands smashing the keyboard. Haley pushed back from the piano, stood, paced.

"I just froze," she said. "He gave me a chance to open up about how he hurt me and I just... I _soft pedaled_ when I could have mashed the accelerator."

Aria busied herself with weaving a blanket of seaweed. We would lay it out on the beach in in the morning; Zeke would lay there, surrounded by lit black candles, while his daughter played his curtain call. In the span of two minutes we'd blitz his body and brain into an unrecognizable puree; a new creature, born of the meat of his bones and tatters of his soul, leaving behind something entirely new. We'd wave goodbye and he'd slip into the ocean, unaware that we'd just taken the last of his humanity. And then we'd all get paid.

She looked at Carmen from over her weaving. "I gave him a little hell, if it makes you feel any better. Carmen over here was too busy polishing his ego to point out how much he hurt you. But you know. Kindness and love and all that.

"Good ol' Carmen, bless her peapickin' heart."

"At least the ritual is easier than what I do with Estelle. No splitting off the side effects, no creature comforts. Just wham, bam, thank ya ma'am, paycheck's in the mail. Kind of relaxing, when you think about it."

Haley lost the thread of her song. Jazz gave way to folk; folk to an etude that cycled, sloppy and rich, in an unending round. Aria put down the carpet and went to join her; as she walked she stared me down, eyes full of fire.

"You still okay with this, Haley? We're basically giving him a get-out-of-jail-free card. He's not even going to remember what he did to you. Maybe the curse'll turn everything topsy-turvy and he'll believe he did the right thing."

Her fingers lifted from the keys. She turned around and leaned back against the upright. "You know, I think I'll live. He's losing everything; the house, white-picket-fence life, second wife, decent retirement. And I think he deserves every moment of that."

"He won't feel that."

"I know he won't." Haley's hands stayed flat on the piano. "I _hated_ him. Still do, deep down, in that pit of your heart that grows heavy when thunderstorms boom overhead, 2 AM, drunk on too much wine. It's just a little pit. A pea at the bottom of a mattress. But it's there. It's been there ten years.

"How _dare_ he come crawling back once I had a chance to make money? All smiles and open arms, pretending like nothing ever happened. I wanted him to _acknowledge_ it. I wanted to scream at him until he got it. I had a _list_, Aria. I rehearsed it in the dorm room mirror. Every Sunday dinner he ruined. Every spanking. Every time he caught me with a dress and made me destroy it myself."

Aria scoffed. "Then _say it_. He's still here. Walk out there right now and say it."

Haley's hand twitched toward the side of the piano. There was a moment — a real moment — where I could see her body going through it, where her shoulders set and her eyes went somewhere ugly. The ears went flat against her head. She was halfway out of her seat.

Then she stopped. She put her hand back on the keys.

"And what then?" Her voice was wrecked. "He doesn't remember the dresses. He doesn't remember the chip system. He's _here_ because he can't stand being in the body that did it. I scream at him for an hour and the only thing I get out of it is the sound of my own screaming. He's already gone, Aria. There's nothing left to punish.

Aria scoffed. "Don't tell me you're going to play that card. After everything we went through, all the work we did to get you back on an even keel, you're just going to shrug, walk away." She whipped around to me, her face scrunched up in white-hot anger. She screamed now: "You did this to her. You and your prioritizing getting along against getting your fair share. You never had principles. Never had _convictions._ Second someone raises their voice you fold like a wet paper bag, and now you have Haley doing it? It makes me _sick!_"

"What do you do with the mad that you feel?" Haley turned back to the piano and played the tune, a smile crossing her face. "Ever watch Mister Rogers? My parents put it on every day. Can't remember much of it - too many bad memories ago - but I came back around to this song. It's just about _stopping_ when you have that evil thought in your mind, you know? All that anger, all those vile plans, and you can just... you can choose to stop, whenever you want to.

"I just want to stop," she said, a hitch in her voice. "The mad, the sad, the feeling that this is all _my fault_, that I did this to him by just being me. It's been years and it just. Won't. Stop."

Aria opened her mouth to respond but Haley just kept playing, head bobbing, her fingers and her voice lost to the song. I walked over and took Aria by the arm; she didn't fight back, didn't scream, didn't shout. She just backpedaled, foot over foot, across the dance floor and back to the round table over which she had draped the seaweed blanket. She wove; I put a hand on her shoulder. Squeezed.

The music trailed off; Haley scribbled some notes on a piece of paper, rolled it up into a tube, and smacked it against her hand a few times. When she stood up she wore a thin, measured smile. "But now he's... he's being punished for sins I tried let go of. I have to live with that because it was done in my name and nobody thought to fucking _ask me_."

She walked over toward us, paper in hand. "It's all in the past now. Dad needs me and I'm going to... I'm going to at least try to make something of this. Because I can _stop_, Aria. Because I want to stop."

"Happened to me. Gertrude too. Nearly happened to your daughter, back in school. Had a pain doll of you made up and everything."

His eyes widened. I shrugged at him.
